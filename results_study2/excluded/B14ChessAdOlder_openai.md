A game of chess offers much more than a pleasant way to pass an afternoon. With a board, a set of pieces and a willing partner, it can bring mental activity, friendly company and the satisfaction of learning something new. There is no need to be an expert, and there is certainly no age limit on enjoying the game. Whether you played years ago or have never moved a pawn, chess can be a rewarding addition to everyday life.

One of its great attractions is the way it gets the mind working. Each move invites you to look carefully, consider your choices and think about what might happen next. You practise concentration as you follow the game, memory as you remember how the pieces move, and problem-solving as you work out a plan. These are useful mental challenges, offered in an enjoyable setting rather than as a test. Chess is not a proven way to prevent dementia, but it can be one worthwhile part of a varied, mentally active life.

Just as valuable is the company that comes with it. A regular game with a neighbour, a friend or someone at a local club gives you a reason to meet, talk and share a little friendly competition. For anyone who spends a lot of time alone, that regular contact can be especially welcome. The conversation may begin with the position on the board and soon turn to gardens, grandchildren or the news. Sometimes the game is simply a comfortable way to spend time together, without any pressure to keep a conversation going.

Chess can also bring different generations together. A grandparent and grandchild can meet across the same board, each with something to offer. Experience and patience may be matched against curiosity and bold ideas. Teaching someone a simple move, or discovering a clever move they have found, creates shared moments that belong to both players. Winning is enjoyable, of course, but it is only one small part of the pleasure.

There is satisfaction, too, in making progress at your own pace. Learning to protect a piece, spotting a threat or finishing a game with a thoughtful plan can provide a genuine sense of achievement. A mistake need not spoil the occasion; it can become something interesting to discuss and try differently next time. Chess encourages patience and flexibility, because even a careful plan sometimes needs changing.

Getting started need not be expensive or complicated. A basic set is enough, and large, clearly marked pieces may make play more comfortable. Games can be short and informal, with breaks whenever needed and no clock unless both players want one. A library, community centre or seniors’ group may offer a welcoming place to learn. For those who prefer playing at home, online games are another option, provided the controls feel comfortable. The important thing is to find a pace and a partner that suit you. A little challenge, a little companionship and something to look forward to can make chess a very welcome habit.
